The Quiet Economics of Attention

Attention is a resource with unusual properties. Unlike money, it cannot be stored. Unlike time, it cannot be reclaimed once spent. Unlike energy, it is not conserved. Economists who study human behavior increasingly describe attention as the scarcest input in the modern digital economy, one that apps, feeds, and notification systems compete for continuously.

Several researchers have proposed that the market for attention behaves like a commons. When every application optimizes for engagement, the shared pool of human focus is depleted. Each party rationally maximizes its own claim while the collective stock degrades. The result is a system in which content is engineered to be habitual rather than useful.

One countervailing design philosophy treats the user's focus as borrowed property. Interfaces that ask for less, disclose interruptions explicitly, and return control quickly tend to produce higher trust over time, even if their immediate engagement metrics are lower. This tension between short-term capture and long-term trust is the central design problem of the next decade.

There is some evidence that deliberate friction can be protective. Systems that slow down the user slightly before destructive actions, that require confirmation before irreversible commitments, and that make the cost of attention explicit, are associated with more considered decisions. Friction is not always an enemy of usability; it is a tool for allocating scarce cognitive resources.

What does this mean for the future of personal computing? Perhaps the machine should not strive to be captivating. Perhaps the most respectful computer is one that waits quietly, responds precisely, and lets the human decide when to engage. Agentic software, increasingly capable of acting on the user's behalf, will need such restraint most of all.

The lesson is not that engagement is bad. The lesson is that the user should remain the author of their own attention. Tools that preserve authorship—whether of attention, of words, or of decisions—are the ones that last.
